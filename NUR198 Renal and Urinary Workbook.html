Unit 1 · The Kidneys: Why Should We Care? 🫘

Before you learn the diseases — the kidneys have ONE job: keep the body in balance.

The six jobs. Every renal disease in this module is one of these six jobs failing. Learn the six, and the signs and symptoms write themselves.

Kidney functionWhat happens if the kidneys stop doing itWhat you'd see in your patient
Filter wastes Urea, creatinine and other nitrogenous wastes stay in the blood — uremia. BUN and creatinine climb, GFR falls. Confusion, lethargy, nausea, metallic taste, itching, uremic breath (fetor), later pericarditis
Remove excess fluid Water is retained in the vascular space → volume overload Weight gain, edema, crackles, JVD, hypertension, dyspnea, low urine output
Balance potassium K⁺ is not excreted → hyperkalemia (>5.0 mEq/L) Peaked T waves → wide QRS, bradycardia, muscle weakness, cramps, cardiac arrest
Maintain acid–base balance H⁺ is not excreted and bicarbonate is not regenerated → metabolic acidosis Kussmaul respirations (deep & fast, blowing off CO₂), lethargy, low pH and low HCO₃⁻, and acidosis pushes K⁺ out of cells making hyperkalemia worse
Produce erythropoietin Bone marrow gets no signal to make red cells → normocytic anemia Fatigue, pallor, dyspnea on exertion, tachycardia, low H&H
Activate vitamin D No calcitriol → calcium is not absorbed from the gut → hypocalcemia + high phosphorus → PTH rises and pulls calcium out of bone Bone pain, pathologic fractures, muscle cramps, positive Chvostek/Trousseau, itching from calcium–phosphate deposits

🩺 Think Like a Nurse

Your patient has crackles, +3 edema, weight gain, and urine output of 80 mL today. You don't know the diagnosis yet.

  • What kidney function has failed? Fluid removal. The kidney is not excreting water, so volume is being held in the vascular space and pushed into the interstitium and the lungs.
  • What assessment finding worries you the most? The crackles — that is fluid in the alveoli, which is an airway/breathing problem.
  • Why? Edema and weight gain are uncomfortable; pulmonary edema kills. ABCs put oxygenation ahead of everything else, so crackles + 80 mL of urine in a day means this patient is heading toward respiratory failure unless fluid comes off.

Also note: 80 mL in 24 hours is anuria (<100 mL/24 hr). Oliguria is <400 mL/24 hr, or less than 0.5 mL/kg/hr.

⛔ Stop & Think — which problem could kill your patient FIRST?

  • ☐ Elevated BUN — ❌ Makes the patient feel awful (nausea, confusion) but does not stop the heart today.
  • ☐ Elevated creatinine — ❌ Tells you how injured the kidney is. It is a marker, not the killer.
  • ☐ Hyperkalemia — ✅ THIS ONE. Potassium changes cardiac membrane excitability — lethal dysrhythmias can occur within minutes.
  • ☐ Anemia — ❌ Chronic and treatable with epoetin alfa and iron; it is a "weeks" problem, not a "minutes" problem.

Renal rule #1: the lab that kills first is potassium.

Mini NCLEX — A client with acute kidney injury develops peaked T waves on telemetry. Which action has the highest priority?
  1. Restrict fluids
  2. Assess bowel sounds
  3. Notify the provider immediately
  4. Encourage oral fluids
Show the answer

C — Notify the provider immediately. Peaked T waves mean the hyperkalemia has already reached the myocardium. You need orders now: IV calcium gluconate to stabilize the cardiac membrane, then insulin with dextrose (and/or albuterol) to drive K⁺ into cells, then a binder or dialysis to actually remove it. Stay with the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring while you call.

A — Restrict fluids: appropriate long-term in AKI, but it does nothing about the potassium sitting on the heart right now.
B — Assess bowel sounds: hypoactive bowel sounds go with hypokalemia; irrelevant to the emergency.
D — Encourage oral fluids: actively harmful — this patient is oliguric and can't excrete extra volume.

Unit 3 · The Renal Detective 🔎

Look only at the assessment. Can you figure out what's happening?

Patient #1 — 82 y/o, vomiting 4 days, BP 82/46, HR 124, UOP 15 mL/hr, BUN ↑, Cr ↑, K⁺ 5.0

  • ☐ Prerenal — ✅ Prerenal AKI. Four days of vomiting = volume loss → hypotension → the kidney is not being perfused.
  • ☐ Intrarenal — ❌ Nothing here is toxic to or inflaming the kidney tissue itself.
  • ☐ Postrenal — ❌ No obstruction: no distended bladder, no BPH history, no stone.

Evidence? Hypotension (82/46) + tachycardia (124, compensating) + dry losses + oliguria (15 mL/hr is well under 0.5 mL/kg/hr). Classic prerenal picture: BUN rises out of proportion to creatinine — a BUN:creatinine ratio above about 20:1, because the slow tubular flow lets urea be reabsorbed.

Patient #2 — Received IV contrast yesterday; creatinine doubled overnight; urine output down

  • Diagnosis? Intrarenal AKI — contrast-induced nephropathy / acute tubular necrosis (ATN)
  • Why? Iodinated contrast is directly toxic to the tubular cells and causes renal vasoconstriction, so the damage is inside the kidney. It typically shows up 24–72 hours after the scan. Prevention: hydrate with IV isotonic fluid before and after, use the smallest dye load, and hold nephrotoxins such as NSAIDs, aminoglycosides and metformin around the study.

Patient #3 — History of BPH, distended bladder, cannot urinate, pain 8/10

  • Diagnosis? Postrenal AKI from acute urinary retention caused by the enlarged prostate obstructing the urethra
  • What intervention comes FIRST? Relieve the obstruction — bladder scan and then insert a urinary catheter per order to drain the bladder. Nothing else works until urine can leave; the back-pressure is what is destroying the kidney, and draining it also fixes the 8/10 pain.

⚠️ After you drain: Watch for post-obstructive diuresis — huge urine volumes over the next hours with hypotension, hypokalemia and hyponatremia. Monitor I&O, BP and electrolytes closely.

Notice: you haven't memorized anything — you reasoned. That is exactly what NCLEX wants.

Unit 4 · Renal Diagnostics 🧪 — What Are the Kidneys Trying to Tell You?

The kidney is a coffee filter. If the filter clogs, the waste doesn't leave — it stays in the bloodstream. That is why kidney disease causes abnormal labs.

Lab Detective

Lab (normal)What does it measure?A HIGH value usually means…Why?
BUN
10–20 mg/dL		 Urea nitrogen — the nitrogen waste from protein breakdown Reduced filtration or a non-renal cause Rises with dehydration, GI bleeding, high protein intake, steroids, catabolism — so a high BUN alone does not prove kidney disease. Falls with fluid overload and liver failure.
Creatinine
0.6–1.2 mg/dL (women slightly lower)		 Waste from muscle metabolism, produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidney The kidney is no longer filtering effectively Because production is constant, a rising creatinine can only mean falling clearance. It is the best single indicator of kidney function. A doubling of creatinine = roughly a 50% loss of GFR.
GFR
>90 mL/min/1.73 m²		 How many mL of plasma the glomeruli filter per minute — the actual filtering capacity A high GFR is not the worry; a LOW GFR is. <60 for 3 months = CKD; <15 = end-stage, dialysis territory. GFR is what CKD is staged on. It falls as nephrons are lost, and it drives drug dosing.
Potassium
3.5–5.0 mEq/L		 The main intracellular cation; controls cardiac and neuromuscular membrane excitability Hyperkalemia — the kidney cannot excrete K⁺ The kidney excretes ~90% of daily potassium. Add metabolic acidosis (H⁺ moves into cells, K⁺ moves out) and cell breakdown, and levels climb fast. This is the value that can kill within minutes.
Phosphorus
3.0–4.5 mg/dL		 Serum phosphate — moves inversely with calcium Hyperphosphatemia from failure to excrete phosphate Phosphate is renally excreted. As it rises it binds calcium, dropping serum calcium, which triggers PTH → bone resorption. Also causes the itching and the red eyes of CKD.
Calcium
9.0–10.5 mg/dL (total)		 Serum calcium — needed for bone, clotting, muscle and cardiac conduction In renal patients calcium is usually LOW, not high Two reasons: no activated vitamin D (so no gut absorption) and high phosphorus binding it up. Low calcium → tetany, cramps, positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, prolonged QT.

⭐ Nursing Pearl

Creatinine is the BEST indicator of kidney function. Unlike BUN, it is barely affected by hydration status, diet, or GI bleeding — it comes from muscle at a steady rate, so a rise means the filter has failed.

Let's Practice — BUN 42, Creatinine 3.1, GFR 18, K⁺ 6.2

  • What concerns you FIRST? The potassium of 6.2 mEq/L.
  • Why? It is the only value on that list that can cause a fatal dysrhythmia in the next few minutes. Get a 12-lead/telemetry, look for peaked T waves, and expect calcium gluconate, insulin + dextrose, and a plan for removal (binder or dialysis). The GFR of 18 tells you this is stage 4–5 disease — important, but not the emergency.

⛔ Stop and Think — which value means immediate risk of sudden death?

  • ☐ BUN — ❌ Reflects waste build-up and hydration; causes symptoms, not arrest.
  • ☐ Creatinine — ❌ Best measure of function, but a high number does not itself stop the heart.
  • ☐ Potassium — ✅ Yes. Hyperkalemia depolarizes the cardiac membrane → peaked T waves, wide QRS, VF/asystole.
  • ☐ GFR — ❌ Stages the disease and guides drug dosing; a chronic number, not an acute threat.
NCLEX Corner — A nurse receives four laboratory reports. Which result requires immediate intervention?
  1. Creatinine 2.1 mg/dL
  2. Potassium 6.4 mEq/L
  3. BUN 45 mg/dL
  4. GFR 32 mL/min
Show the answer

B — Potassium 6.4 mEq/L. Because it is the only value on the page with a cardiac deadline. Assess the patient, get the patient on a monitor, and call.

A — Creatinine 2.1: abnormal and important to trend, but expected in renal disease.
C — BUN 45: high; explains the nausea and confusion; not lethal today.
D — GFR 32: stage 3b CKD. Chronic. Adjust drug doses, don't run down the hall.

Clinical Judgment — "The patient's creatinine doubled overnight." What questions pop into your head?

  • 1. Is the patient making urine? How many mL in the last hour and the last 24 hours?
  • 2. What did we do to them? Any IV contrast, NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, ACE inhibitors, or a hypotensive episode in the last 48 hours?
  • 3. What is the potassium (and the ECG)? Doubling creatinine means K⁺ and acid are being retained too.
  • 4. Is it volume? What are the BP, HR, weight, and I&O trend — are they dry (prerenal) or overloaded?
  • 5. Could this be obstruction? Bladder scan — a distended bladder is fixable in five minutes.

🩺 Think Like the Nurse — "My back really hurts," right over the costovertebral angle

  • Why does this matter? CVA tenderness means the pain is over the kidney, not the bladder. The infection has ascended — think pyelonephritis, which is a systemic infection that can scar the kidney and seed the bloodstream.
  • ⛔ What assessment should you perform next? Take a full set of vital signs — temperature, HR, BP, RR and mental status — looking for the sepsis picture (fever, HR >100, SBP <100, confusion). Then percuss the CVA gently and check the urine.

Clinical Case — Mrs. Wilson, 79, "she just isn't acting like herself"

Temp 100.9°F · confused · weak · urinary frequency · BP 100/62 · HR 112

  • Q1 — What should the nurse suspect FIRST? A urinary tract infection, likely already ascending — and possible early urosepsis. Get a urinalysis with culture, blood cultures, and vitals.
  • Q2 — Why is this different from a younger adult? Older adults often do not mount the classic burning/frequency picture or a high fever. Their presenting sign is a change in mental status — new confusion, falls, incontinence, decreased appetite, or generalized weakness. A "normal" temperature can still be a fever for them because their baseline is lower.
  • Q3 — Which finding makes her higher priority? The combination of HR 112 with BP 100/62 and altered mental status. That is early sepsis physiology — she is compensating now, and a falling BP is next.

Urinalysis Detective

Cloudy · nitrites positive · leukocyte esterase positive · WBC elevated · bacteria present · SG 1.025 · trace protein

  • Most likely diagnosis? Bacterial urinary tract infection. Positive nitrites = gram-negative bacteria (usually E. coli) converting dietary nitrate; positive leukocyte esterase + WBCs = the inflammatory response.
  • Would you expect antibiotics? YES / NO YES — this is a symptomatic bacterial infection. Culture first, then antibiotics. Note SG 1.025 is normal-to-concentrated (normal 1.005–1.030) and trace protein is not alarming here.

Priority Nursing Actions — number 1 (first) to 5 (last)

  • 1 — Assess pain (assessment before intervention; also tells you whether this is bladder or flank pain)
  • 2 — Obtain urine culture (must be collected BEFORE the first antibiotic dose)
  • 3 — Administer prescribed antibiotic (treat the infection promptly once the specimen is in hand)
  • 4 — Encourage oral fluids (flushes bacteria; supportive, not urgent)
  • 5 — Teach front-to-back wiping (prevention/teaching is always last when someone is acutely ill)

Why must the culture come before antibiotics? Even one dose can suppress bacterial growth and give a false-negative culture. Without a positive culture and sensitivities you cannot narrow the antibiotic, so the patient stays on broad-spectrum therapy longer than necessary and resistant organisms get missed.

Clinical Judgment Challenge — A hospitalized patient has an indwelling urinary catheter. Which action is MOST effective at preventing CAUTI?
  1. Empty the drainage bag once every shift.
  2. Irrigate the catheter every day.
  3. Maintain a closed drainage system.
  4. Change the catheter every 24 hours.
Show the answer

C — Maintain a closed drainage system. Every break in the system is a doorway for bacteria into a sterile space. Keep the bag below the bladder and off the floor, keep tubing free of dependent loops, secure the catheter, do daily perineal care with soap and water — and above all, remove the catheter as early as possible; duration is the biggest CAUTI risk factor of all.

A — Empty once a shift: too infrequent; empty when 1/2 to 2/3 full, but frequency of emptying isn't the main defense.
B — Irrigate daily: never irrigate routinely — it opens the closed system and pushes organisms upward.
D — Change every 24 hours: routine scheduled changes do not reduce infection and add trauma to the urethra.

Don't stop at "this patient has a UTI." Ask: How sick are they? Has it spread upward? Could this become sepsis? And — the one that matters — what finding would make me call the provider immediately? Answer: fever with hypotension, tachycardia, rising lactate, or new confusion.

Unit 6 · Urinary Incontinence 💧

Don't memorize the types — meet the patients. For each one ask: "Why is this patient leaking urine?"

Patient A — 42 y/o woman: "Every time I laugh, sneeze, or cough, I leak a little."

  • Type? Stress incontinence
  • WHY? Weak pelvic floor muscles and a weak urethral sphincter, usually after childbirth, aging, or estrogen loss. A cough spikes intra-abdominal pressure above what the sphincter can hold, so a small amount escapes. The bladder itself is normal.
  • First-line treatment? Pelvic floor muscle (Kegel) exercises — plus weight loss, avoiding caffeine/alcohol, and timed voiding. Surgery (sling) and pessaries come later.

Patient B — 73 y/o: "When I feel like I have to go, I have about 10 seconds."

  • Type? Urge incontinence (overactive bladder)
  • What is happening inside the bladder? The detrusor muscle contracts involuntarily and too early, before the bladder is actually full — so the urge is sudden, intense, and unstoppable.
  • Nursing intervention? Bladder retraining with a timed voiding schedule, gradually lengthening the interval; cut caffeine, alcohol and artificial sweeteners; keep a path to the toilet clear; anticholinergics such as oxybutynin or tolterodine, or mirabegron, if prescribed.

Patient C — 82 y/o man with BPH: "I always feel full." Dribbles all day. Bladder scan 850 mL.

  • Type? Overflow incontinence
  • Why? The enlarged prostate obstructs outflow. The bladder over-distends, pressure inside exceeds sphincter resistance, and urine dribbles out over the top — while 850 mL stays behind. Leaking urine does NOT mean the bladder is empty.
  • Priority nursing action? Relieve the retention — bladder scan (done: 850 mL) then catheterize per order. Because that retained volume causes back-pressure → hydronephrosis → postrenal AKI, plus a high infection risk.

Patient D — spinal cord injury; bladder empties without warning; no control

  • Type? Reflex incontinence — a neurogenic bladder
  • Why? The spinal cord lesion cuts the communication between bladder and brain. The bladder fills and the reflex arc fires a detrusor contraction at a set volume, with no sensation and no voluntary control. Managed with a scheduled program of intermittent catheterization.

Patient E — severe arthritis; can't unbutton pants fast enough

  • Is the bladder working normally? YES / NO YES. The urinary system is intact.
  • Then why is the patient incontinent? This is functional incontinence — a mobility, dexterity, cognition or environment problem, not a bladder problem. Fix the environment: elastic waistbands and Velcro, a bedside commode, a call light within reach, good lighting, clear pathways, and scheduled toileting.

Patient F — new incontinence after starting a diuretic; resolves when the drug is adjusted

  • Type? Transient (temporary) incontinence — caused by a reversible trigger. Remember DIAPPERS: Delirium, Infection, Atrophic vaginitis, Pharmaceuticals, Psychological, Excess urine output, Restricted mobility, Stool impaction.
🩺 Think Like a Nurse — Which patient requires the nurse's attention FIRST?
  1. Stress incontinence
  2. Overflow incontinence with bladder distention
  3. Functional incontinence
  4. Urge incontinence
Show the answer

B — Overflow incontinence with bladder distention. Because this one is causing organ damage right now: retained urine raises pressure back up the ureters → hydronephrosis → postrenal AKI, and stagnant urine breeds infection. It is also painful. Bladder scan and catheterize.

A, C, D are all quality-of-life problems handled with teaching, retraining and environmental changes. None of them threaten kidney function today.

⛔ Stop & Think — no void in 10 hours, palpable bladder, leaking urine

  • Should the nurse assume the bladder is empty? YES / NO NO.
  • Why? Leaking with a palpable bladder is the classic picture of overflow — the bladder is so full it is spilling. Scan it. If a large residual is present, catheterize per order. Assuming "she's voiding, she's fine" is how retention becomes hydronephrosis.

💡 Clinical Pearl: Overflow incontinence fools students. Leaking urine sometimes means the bladder is too full, not empty.

Unit 7 · Bladder Cancer ⚠️

Lots of urinary problems cause blood in the urine. Bladder cancer is different because of HOW the blood shows up.

  • The classic sign is gross, painless, intermittent hematuria — visibly bloody urine with no burning, no colic, no fever
  • Why is this finding concerning? Painless bleeding means nothing is inflamed, obstructed, or spasming — so infection and stones do not explain it. Untreated bladder cancer keeps growing while the patient feels fine, and the bleeding is intermittent, so people talk themselves out of getting checked when it clears up.

Nursing Clue

"My urine has blood in it, but it doesn't hurt." The question that should come to mind: "Does this patient smoke, or have they ever?" — and then: painless hematuria in an adult is bladder cancer until proven otherwise. It needs cystoscopy, not reassurance.

Risk Factors

Risk factorWhy does it increase risk?
Cigarette smokingThe #1 modifiable risk factor. Carcinogens from tobacco are absorbed, filtered by the kidney, and then sit in contact with the bladder lining while urine is stored — a chemical bath on the urothelium. Smokers have roughly 2–4× the risk.
Older ageDecades of accumulated carcinogen exposure plus age-related DNA repair decline. Most cases are diagnosed after age 55.
Male sexMen are diagnosed about 3–4× more often — historically higher smoking and industrial-chemical exposure, plus hormonal differences in the urothelium.
Occupational chemical exposureAromatic amines and dyes — rubber, paint, leather, textile, printing, hairdressing and truck-driving trades. Same mechanism: the chemicals are excreted in urine and pool in the bladder.

⛔ Why do smokers have such a high risk? Because urine is where the body dumps what it detoxifies. Whatever is inhaled ends up dissolved in urine, and that urine sits in the bladder for hours at a time — the bladder gets the longest, most concentrated exposure of any organ downstream of the lungs.

Diagnostic Match-Up

TestPurpose
UrinalysisMay detect blood in the urine — often microscopic hematuria; a screening clue, never the diagnosis

After cystoscopy, teach: pink-tinged urine and mild burning are expected for a day or two; increase fluids; report bright red bleeding with clots, inability to void, fever, or worsening pain.

After the bladder comes out, urine still has to leave the body. The surgeon builds a new road.

Draw the arrows — ileal conduit

StartKidney

2Ileal conduit — a short segment of ileum used as a pipe

Key point: an ileal conduit is incontinent and continuous — there is no storage and no sphincter, so urine drains constantly and the pouch is worn 24/7. Expect mucus in the urine; that is normal because the conduit is made of bowel.

What Does the Nurse Assess? — circle all that apply

  • Stoma color — ✅ the single most important assessment — it tells you whether the tissue is perfused
  • Skin integrity — ✅ alkaline urine excoriates peristomal skin quickly
  • Urine output — ✅ must be at least ~30 mL/hr; no output = obstruction or hypovolemia
  • Pulse quality — ❌ not a stoma assessment (still part of a general post-op check, just not part of this)
  • Pouch seal — ✅ a leaking seal means skin breakdown and odor within hours
  • Edema around stoma — ✅ mild edema is expected for the first few days; increasing edema is not

Healthy or Not? — a stoma should be…

  • ☐ Pale — ❌ suggests anemia or poor perfusion
  • ☐ Black — ❌ necrosis — a surgical emergency
  • ☐ Moist and pink/red — ✅ correct — looks like the inside of your cheek, beefy red and shiny
  • ☐ Dry — ❌ a healthy stoma is always moist; dryness signals compromise

🚨 Critical Thinking — the stoma suddenly turns dark purple

  • Priority concern? Impaired blood supply to the stoma — ischemia progressing toward necrosis. Dusky/purple → black means the tissue is dying and the patient needs surgical revision.
  • What should you do FIRST? Notify the surgeon immediately. Ischemic bowel does not recover on its own and cannot be fixed at the bedside. Meanwhile, remove any constricting pouch/belt, do not apply heat or massage, keep the patient NPO in case of a return to the OR, and document the exact color and time.

NCLEX Priority — One day after creation of an ileal conduit, which finding requires immediate intervention?
  1. Pink stoma
  2. Yellow urine draining
  3. Dark purple stoma
  4. Mild edema around the stoma
Show the answer

C — Dark purple stoma. Purple/dusky = the stoma has lost its blood supply. Call the surgeon now; necrosis requires operative revision.

A — Pink stoma: that is exactly what you want to see.
B — Yellow urine draining: expected — continuous drainage is the whole point of a conduit. (Mucus threads are normal too.)
D — Mild edema: normal in the first several post-op days; it recedes as swelling resolves.

  • 2. When did it start, and is it every void or just at the beginning/end of the stream? Blood throughout suggests a kidney or bladder source.
  • 3. What does it look like — bright red, pink, clots, or cola-colored? Cola/tea colored with edema and hypertension = glomerulonephritis, not bleeding.
  • 4. What else is going on — fever, chills, flank pain, weight loss, trouble voiding?
  • 5. What is your history? Smoking, chemical exposure, anticoagulants, recent procedure or catheter, trauma, strep infection 1–2 weeks ago, beets/rifampin (which just stain the urine).

Unit 9 · Glomerulonephritis — When the Filter Becomes Inflamed 🔥

Normally: blood enters, waste leaves, protein stays, red cells stay. Now the filter develops tiny holes because it is inflamed.

What can now leak through? — circle all that apply

  • □ Water — Water always crosses the filter — that is normal filtration. In GN the problem is that less water gets filtered out, so it is retained.
  • □ Protein — ✅ Yes — but only a moderate amount in GN (usually <3.5 g/day). Massive protein loss belongs to nephrotic syndrome.
  • □ Red blood cells — ✅ Yes — this is the signature. RBCs and RBC casts in the urine turn it cola/tea colored.
  • □ White blood cells — ✅ Yes, WBCs and casts appear because the glomerulus is inflamed.

🩺 Think Like a Nurse

  • If the kidney filter is inflamed, would urine output increase or decrease? Decrease — oliguria.
  • Explain WHY. Inflammation swells and narrows the glomerular capillary loops, so less plasma is filtered per minute (GFR drops). Less filtrate in = less urine out, and the salt and water that should have been excreted stay in the body → edema and hypertension.

Recognizing the Patient — check all that support glomerulonephritis

  • □ Cola-colored urine — ✅ The hallmark — RBCs and RBC casts in the urine

  • □ Oliguria — ✅ GFR has fallen
  • □ Weight gain — ✅ Fluid retention — daily weight is the most reliable measure of it

Unit 10 · Nephrotic Syndrome — When the Filter Leaks Protein 🫧

Finish the Story

STEP 1Damaged glomerulus
➜
STEP 2Protein leaks into urine (>3.5 g/day — massive proteinuria, foamy urine)
➜
STEP 3Blood loses albumin — and with it, plasma oncotic (pulling) pressure
➜
STEP 4Fluid leaves the bloodstream
➜
STEP 5Fluid enters tissues
➜
STEP 6Massive generalized edema (anasarca) — and because the vascular space is now under-filled, RAAS turns on and retains even more salt and water

Why is albumin important? Albumin is a sponge — it pulls water into the bloodstream. If albumin drops, the water leaves the vessels and moves into the interstitial tissue. That is why the swelling is massive yet the intravascular volume can be low: the fluid is in the wrong compartment.

Clinical connection: nephrotic edema is not the kidney "making more fluid" — it is fluid leaving the blood vessels.

Assessment Detective — which patient has nephrotic syndrome?

  • Patient A (cola-colored urine, hypertension, recent strep) — ❌ That is glomerulonephritis — blood is leaking, and there is a post-infectious trigger.
  • Patient B (foamy urine, +4 pitting edema, albumin 2.1, proteinuria) — ✅ Nephrotic syndrome. Foam = protein in the urine; albumin 2.1 g/dL (normal 3.5–5.0) is the hypoalbuminemia; the +4 edema is the direct consequence of losing oncotic pressure.

Fill in the Table

HyperlipidemiaThe liver senses low oncotic pressure and ramps up production of all its proteins, including lipoproteins — and lipid clearance falls at the same time. Result: high cholesterol, high triglycerides, and lipids in the urine

⛔ Why does the liver make extra cholesterol? It is trying to compensate. The liver's response to falling plasma protein is to synthesize more protein — and lipoprotein synthesis rises along with albumin synthesis. It cannot keep up with the albumin loss, so what you actually see is a low albumin and a high cholesterol.

⚠️ The complication students forget: nephrotic syndrome is hypercoagulable — antithrombin III is lost in the urine along with albumin. Watch for DVT, PE, and renal vein thrombosis. Also watch for infection, because immunoglobulins are lost too.

Unit 11 · Acute Kidney Injury 🚨

Acute means it happened fast — hours to days. The good news: AKI is often reversible if the cause is found and treated early.

Where is the problem?

BeforeBlood flow → Prerenal — a perfusion problem; the kidney tissue is still healthy
➜
InsideKidneys → Intrarenal — damage to the kidney tissue itself (tubules, glomeruli, interstitium)
➜
AfterUrine leaves kidneys → bladder → urethra → Postrenal — an obstruction anywhere downstream of the kidney

AKI Detective — Scenario #1 (83 y/o, 4 days vomiting/diarrhea, BP 82/48, HR 122, dry skin, UOP 12 mL/hr)

  • Are the kidneys damaged? YES / NO NO — not yet. The tissue is intact.
  • Then WHY are they failing? They are not being perfused. GI losses dropped the circulating volume, the MAP fell below what the glomeruli need to filter, and filtration stopped. Fix the volume and the kidney works again — but if the hypoperfusion continues, prerenal AKI becomes acute tubular necrosis, and then it IS damage.
  • 🩺 What do the kidneys need before they can filter? Blood flow. Without perfusion, even perfectly healthy kidneys cannot work.

Scenario #2 — IV contrast; creatinine doubles overnight; urine output falls

  • Before / Inside / After? Inside — intrarenal.
  • Explain WHY. Contrast is a nephrotoxin: it constricts renal vessels and is directly toxic to tubular epithelium, producing acute tubular necrosis. The cells slough into the tubules and plug them. Other classic intrarenal causes: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, NSAIDs, rhabdomyolysis (myoglobin), transfusion reactions, glomerulonephritis, and prolonged prerenal ischemia.

Scenario #3 — severe BPH, no urine for 18 hours, distended bladder, creatinine rising

  • Before / Inside / After? After — postrenal. The prostate is blocking outflow, urine backs up, pressure rises to the kidney, and filtration stops. Healthy kidneys cannot make urine if urine cannot leave. Postrenal AKI is the most rapidly reversible type — if you catch it before the nephrons are destroyed.

Phase 1 — Onset. Write three examples of something that damages the kidneys.

  • 1. A hypotensive event — hemorrhage, sepsis, prolonged surgery, or severe dehydration
  • 2. A nephrotoxin — IV contrast, gentamicin, vancomycin, NSAIDs, or chemotherapy
  • 3. An obstruction — BPH with retention, bilateral stones, or a blocked catheter

What assessment finding is MOST important during this phase? Urine output — hourly. A falling output (<0.5 mL/kg/hr or <30 mL/hr) is the earliest sign, and it appears before the creatinine moves. Catching it here is what makes AKI reversible.

Phase 2 — Oliguric phase. Fill in the blanks.

  • Urine output becomes low — oliguric, <400 mL/24 hr (or <0.5 mL/kg/hr); sometimes anuric <100 mL/24 hr
  • Fluid begins to accumulate — weight gain, edema, JVD, hypertension
  • Potassium becomes elevated — hyperkalemia, the killer of this phase
  • Creatinine becomes elevated — and keeps climbing daily
  • BUN becomes elevated — azotemia → nausea, confusion, itching
  • The lungs may develop crackles → pulmonary edema
  • The ECG may show tall peaked T waves, then a flattened P wave and widened QRS
  • Bonus — acid–base: metabolic acidosis (retained H⁺, lost bicarbonate) → Kussmaul respirations, and the acidosis drives potassium even higher
  • Bonus — sodium: dilutional hyponatremia — the sodium looks low because it is swimming in retained water

⛔ Why is hyperkalemia the priority during this phase? Because it is the only abnormality here with a cardiac deadline. The kidney excretes ~90% of daily potassium; with no urine, potassium climbs while acidosis pushes even more K⁺ out of the cells. Peaked T waves → wide QRS → VF or asystole can happen in minutes, while the high BUN and edema unfold over days.

Clinical Picture — weight gain, +3 edema, crackles, BP 186/98, UOP 180 mL today, K⁺ 6.5

What is your FIRST concern? The potassium of 6.5 mEq/L. Put the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring, get a 12-lead, and notify the provider — anticipate IV calcium gluconate (cardiac membrane protection), then insulin + D50 and/or albuterol to shift K⁺ into cells, then sodium polystyrene sulfonate/patiromer or urgent dialysis to remove it. Crackles + 180 mL/day is the close second, and dialysis fixes both.

Prioritize! — number the interventions

  • 1 — Monitor ECG (with K⁺ 6.5 this is the assessment that detects lethal change in real time)
  • 2 — Review potassium level (confirms and trends the threat, and guides treatment)
  • 3 — Assess lung sounds (breathing — crackles mean overload has reached the alveoli)
  • 4 — Assess for edema (part of the volume picture, but peripheral edema is not immediately dangerous)
  • 5 — Strict intake and output (essential ongoing monitoring; results accumulate over hours)
  • 6 — Obtain daily weight (the best fluid measure, but it is a once-a-day trend, not a rescue)

Which intervention could save the patient's life TODAY? Cardiac monitoring plus acting on the potassium — recognizing peaked T waves and getting calcium gluconate, insulin/dextrose, and dialysis started.

Unit 12 · The Diuretic Phase — The Fluid Management FLIP 🔄

Students think: "The patient is peeing again, so they're better." Not so fast.

🩺 Think like the kidney — can they concentrate urine yet? YES / NO NO. The tubules recover their ability to filter before they recover their ability to concentrate. So the patient makes huge volumes of very dilute urine (specific gravity around 1.010) and dumps water and electrolytes they cannot afford to lose.

Recovery Phase

How long? Weeks to months — sometimes up to a year. GFR climbs back toward baseline, but some patients are left with a permanently reduced GFR and go on to develop CKD.

Reflection — why is AKI potentially reversible while CKD is not? In AKI the nephrons are stunned or acutely injured, not gone — tubular cells can regenerate once the cause (low perfusion, toxin, obstruction) is removed. In CKD the nephrons are progressively destroyed and replaced by fibrous scar tissue, and nephrons do not regenerate. Treatment in CKD slows the loss; it does not restore it.

100 workers in a factory. One quits, the other 99 work harder. Then another. Then another. Eventually there aren't enough workers left to run the factory. That is CKD.

Remember: CKD is progressive and irreversible — treatment slows progression, it does not cure. Definition: kidney damage or GFR <60 mL/min for 3 months or longer.

Why do people develop CKD? — which chronic diseases damage blood vessels?

  • □ Diabetes — ✅ #1 cause. Chronic hyperglycemia thickens and scars the glomerular capillaries (diabetic nephropathy). Earliest sign: microalbuminuria.
  • □ Hypertension — ✅ #2 cause. High pressure sclerose the small renal arterioles — and the damaged kidney then raises BP further, a vicious cycle.
  • □ Lupus — ✅ Yes — lupus nephritis is immune-complex damage to the glomerulus
  • □ Recurrent kidney infections — ✅ Yes — repeated pyelonephritis scars renal tissue permanently
  • □ Polycystic kidney disease — ✅ Yes — the leading inherited cause; cysts replace functioning nephrons

🩺 Which two diseases cause MOST cases, and why? Diabetes and hypertension — together roughly two-thirds of all CKD. Both are diseases of the blood vessels, and the kidney is essentially a filter made of a million tiny blood vessels. Whatever damages vessels systemically damages nephrons. This is also why BP and glucose control are the two interventions that most slow CKD progression.

Clinical connection: a CKD patient often comes to the hospital for something other than their kidneys — heart failure, confusion, shortness of breath, weakness, or a fall. Ask: if the kidneys can't do their job, which body system will struggle next?

The Nursing Process — what will you monitor every shift?

  • ☐ Weight — ✅ Best single fluid indicator — same scale, same time, after voiding

  • ☐ Blood pressure — ✅ Both cause and consequence of kidney damage; control slows progression

Dialysis does not fix the kidneys. It takes over some of their jobs — not all of them.

What can dialysis actually do?

Clinical Reasoning — who benefits from peritoneal dialysis?

  • □ Lives several hours from a dialysis center — ✅ PD is done at home, so no thrice-weekly travel

⛔ STOP — the dialysate suddenly becomes cloudy

  • First thought? PERITONITIS — cloudy effluent is the earliest and most reliable sign, and it usually appears before the fever.
  • Why is this an emergency? The peritoneum is a huge, highly vascular surface with a catheter running straight into it from the outside. Send effluent for cell count, Gram stain and culture, then give antibiotics — often added directly into the dialysate.

Assessment Detective — your patient has an AV fistula

  • I should FEEL a THRILL — a buzzing/purring vibration under your fingertips (palpate)
  • I should HEAR a BRUIT — a swishing, whooshing sound with the bell of the stethoscope (auscultate)
  • What if they're missing? The access is clotted or occluded. Also check for a cool, pale, numb or painful hand distal to the fistula — that is steal syndrome, where the fistula is diverting blood away from the hand.
  • What does no thrill mean? Blood is not flowing through the fistula — it has thrombosed. Without it, the patient has no way to receive dialysis.
  • What should the nurse do? Notify the provider/dialysis team immediately — a clotted access is a time-sensitive problem; declotting or thrombectomy may still save it. Meanwhile protect the arm: no BP, no sticks, no constriction, and document the exact time you last felt a thrill.

Before Dialysis — check all that apply

  • ☐ Weight — ✅ Pre-weight vs. dry weight determines how much fluid to pull off
  • ☐ Blood pressure — ✅ Baseline for detecting the hypotension that fluid removal causes
  • ☐ Potassium — ✅ Drives urgency and dialysate composition
  • ☐ Medication list — ✅ Some drugs must be held; some must be redosed after
  • ☐ Lung sounds — ✅ Baseline volume status
  • ☐ Access site — ✅ Thrill, bruit, and signs of infection — no access, no treatment
  • Which medications are commonly held before dialysis? Antihypertensives (and often other BP-lowering drugs), plus water-soluble medications that dialysis will simply remove — many antibiotics, water-soluble vitamins, and digoxin needs special attention.
  • Why? Dialysis itself drops the blood pressure by pulling off several liters of fluid. Giving an antihypertensive first stacks two BP-lowering effects and causes symptomatic hypotension mid-treatment. And any drug that crosses the dialyzer is being thrown away — the patient gets no therapeutic benefit, so those doses are given after the run.

During Dialysis — the blood pressure suddenly drops

  • Why might this happen? Fluid is being removed from the vascular space faster than it can refill from the tissues → hypovolemia. Made worse by taking antihypertensives beforehand, eating during treatment (blood shunts to the gut), too aggressive an ultrafiltration goal, or an inaccurate dry weight. Also consider bleeding from the access and, rarely, a transfusion-type or air-embolism reaction.
  • What assessment should you perform FIRST? Recheck the blood pressure and assess the patient's level of consciousness and symptoms (dizziness, nausea, cramping, pallor, diaphoresis). Then lower the head of the bed / Trendelenburg, slow or stop ultrafiltration, and give a normal saline bolus per protocol to restore volume.

⛔ STOP & THINK — nausea → headache → confusion during dialysis

  • What complication comes to mind? Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome. The classic progression is nausea → headache → restlessness → confusion → twitching → seizures. Most common during the first few treatments or when the BUN was very high.
  • Why is this dangerous? It is cerebral edema. Urea leaves the blood quickly but lingers in brain tissue, so water is pulled into brain cells. Untreated it progresses to seizures, coma and death. Slow or stop the dialysis and notify the provider now.

Unit 16 · Renal Transplantation 🫱

A transplant is life-changing — but it is not a cure. The body sees the new kidney as foreign and will try to attack it.

⛔ Stop & Think

  • If your immune system attacks the new kidney, what is this called? Rejection. Hyperacute occurs within minutes to 48 hours (the organ must be removed); acute occurs within about the first week to 3 months and is often treatable; chronic occurs over months to years and gradually destroys the graft.
  • How do we prevent it? Careful donor–recipient matching (ABO and HLA, plus a crossmatch) and lifelong immunosuppressant therapy — typically a calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine or tacrolimus) + an antiproliferative (mycophenolate) + a corticosteroid.

Build the Story

1Healthy donor kidney
➜
2Surgery — the new kidney is placed in the iliac fossa (lower abdomen); the old kidneys usually stay in
➜
3Body recognizes new kidney as foreign
➜
4Immune system mounts an attack against the graft (rejection)
➜
5Patient must take immunosuppressants for life

What if they stop taking them? The immune system reactivates and rejects the graft — the kidney fails and the patient goes back on dialysis, having burned a scarce organ. Nonadherence is one of the leading causes of late graft loss, so assess for cost barriers, side effects (hirsutism, gum overgrowth, tremor, weight gain, mood changes) and understanding at every visit.

Nursing Priorities after Kidney Transplant — check them off

  • ☐ Monitor urine output — ✅ The #1 indicator of graft function. Hourly at first; a sudden decrease is an early sign of rejection or of obstruction/thrombosis.
  • ☐ Monitor for infection — ✅ Immunosuppression makes infection the leading cause of death after transplant — and the signs are blunted
  • ☐ Encourage medication adherence — ✅ Lifelong, on schedule, never skipped — missed doses cost the kidney
  • ☐ Teach low-sodium diet — ✅ Supports blood pressure control and protects the new kidney (steroids also cause sodium retention)
  • ☐ Assess incision — ✅ Steroids and immunosuppressants delay healing and mask redness; watch for dehiscence and leakage
  • ☐ Encourage incentive spirometer — ✅ Prevents atelectasis and pneumonia — a critical concern when the immune system is suppressed
  • ☐ Monitor creatinine — ✅ A rising creatinine is one of the earliest laboratory signs of rejection
  • ☐ Monitor temperature — ✅ Even a low-grade fever is significant — it may mean rejection or infection, and both are emergencies here

Why Are Transplant Patients So Vulnerable?

  • Immunosuppressants decrease the body's ability to mount an immune response — to recognize and fight invading organisms and abnormal cells
  • This places the patient at risk for infection (bacterial, viral — CMV, BK virus, herpes — and fungal), reactivation of latent infections such as TB, and long-term malignancy, especially skin cancer and lymphoma. Teach: avoid crowds and sick contacts, meticulous hand hygiene, no live vaccines, food safety, daily sunscreen, and report ANY fever right away.

Unit 17 · Polycystic Kidney Disease — When Cysts Replace Healthy Kidneys 🧬

Not an infection. Not a lifestyle disease. It is inherited — most commonly autosomal dominant, so each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance.

Big Picture

1Healthy nephrons
➜
2Fluid-filled cysts develop
➜
3Kidneys enlarge
➜
4Healthy tissue is replaced
➜
5Kidney function progressively declines → CKD → end-stage renal disease, usually by age 50–60

What Makes PKD Different?

  • As cysts grow they squeeze the tiny vessels inside the kidney. What happens when blood flow decreases? The juxtaglomerular cells sense low renal perfusion — exactly as they would in hemorrhage — and switch on the RAAS.
  • So the kidneys release: RENIN → angiotensin I → (ACE) angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor → aldosterone, which retains sodium and water; ADH is released too.
  • What happens next? Blood pressure RISES — and the hypertension then damages the remaining nephrons, which further reduces perfusion, which releases more renin. A self-feeding loop.

⭐ Dr. K's Clinical Tip: the hypertension in PKD is not stress — it is RAAS activation caused by cysts compressing renal blood vessels, and it is one of the most tested concepts in this unit.

Which Pain Is It?

  • Dull, aching flank pain — Chronic pain from enlarging cysts stretching the renal capsule. Expected; managed with heat and non-nephrotoxic analgesia.
  • Sharp, severe flank pain — Something acute: a ruptured cyst, a kidney stone, or an infected cyst. Look for new hematuria, fever, and hypotension — and report it.

🩺 Why is blood pressure the MOST important thing to monitor in PKD?

Because controlling blood pressure is the one intervention that measurably slows the loss of kidney function. Uncontrolled hypertension accelerates nephron destruction, and PKD patients also carry a high rate of cerebral (berry) aneurysms — a hypertensive crisis can rupture one. So BP control protects both the kidneys and the brain. ACE inhibitors/ARBs are first line.

Nursing Priorities — rank highest to lowest

  • 1 — Monitor blood pressure (the intervention that changes the disease trajectory and prevents aneurysm rupture)
  • 2 — Monitor renal labs (creatinine, BUN, GFR, K⁺ — detects decline and dangerous electrolytes)
  • 3 — Assess pain (comfort, and a change in the pain pattern signals rupture, stone or infection)
  • 4 — Monitor daily weights (fluid status as function declines)
  • 5 — Encourage low-sodium diet (essential long-term teaching that supports BP control — but teaching ranks below monitoring an unstable parameter)

Reasoning: Assessment and monitoring of the parameter that can cause acute harm come before comfort measures, and long-term teaching always ranks last on a priority list.

🚨 Clinical Judgment — worsening headaches, blurred vision, BP 198/112

  • What complication concerns you most? A hypertensive emergency — and specifically, in PKD, rupture of a cerebral (berry) aneurysm or hypertensive encephalopathy/stroke. Headache + visual changes + a diastolic over 110 is end-organ damage in progress.
  • What should the nurse do FIRST? Notify the provider immediately and prepare for rapid, controlled BP reduction. Because this pressure is actively damaging the brain, eyes and kidneys right now. Meanwhile: keep the patient quiet with the head of bed elevated, do a neuro check, recheck the BP in both arms, and have seizure precautions and IV access ready.

⛔ Why do we push fluids? Dilute urine keeps minerals in solution so they cannot crystallize, and the volume helps flush an existing stone downstream. Target roughly 2.5–3 L/day unless the patient is fluid restricted — enough that the urine stays pale and clear.

🩺 What nursing interventions help a small stone pass? Clinical connection — why can't they sit still? Renal colic is visceral pain from a spasming, distended ureter, and no position relieves it — so the patient keeps moving looking for one. Compare this with peritonitis, where movement stretches the inflamed peritoneum and makes the pain worse, so those patients lie perfectly still with their knees drawn up. Writhing = stone. Frozen still = peritonitis.

🚨 Hyperkalemia Rescue — the one sequence to memorize

This shows up in AKI, CKD, dialysis and transplant questions. Learn the order once and it answers all of them.

STEP 1 — PROTECTIV calcium gluconate — stabilizes the cardiac membrane. Does not lower K⁺. Given first when ECG changes are present.
➜
STEP 2 — SHIFTRegular insulin + D50, nebulized albuterol, and sodium bicarbonate if acidotic — move K⁺ into the cells. Temporary; watch glucose.
➜
STEP 3 — REMOVEDialysis (definitive and fastest), or a binder such as sodium polystyrene sulfonate/patiromer, or a loop diuretic if the patient still makes urine.

ECG progression to recognize: tall peaked T waves → prolonged PR → flattened/absent P waves → widened QRS → sine wave → VF/asystole. Peaked T waves = act now.

Diet teaching — high-potassium foods to limit: bananas, oranges and orange juice, potatoes (including sweet potatoes), tomatoes and tomato sauce, spinach and other greens, avocado, melons, dried fruit, beans and legumes, nuts, milk, and salt substitutes (they are potassium chloride — the one people forget). Lower-potassium choices: white rice, pasta, bread, apples and applesauce, berries, grapes, green beans, cabbage, cauliflower.

Answer first, then open the rationale. The reasoning matters more than the letter.

1. A client with CKD is receiving epoetin alfa. Which laboratory value should the nurse review to evaluate the therapeutic effect?
  1. Potassium
  2. Hemoglobin
  3. Creatinine
  4. Phosphorus
Show the answer

B — Hemoglobin. Epoetin alfa replaces erythropoietin, so its job is to raise red cell production. Target roughly 10–11 g/dL; exceeding ~11–12 raises the risk of hypertension, stroke and thrombosis.

A — Potassium: monitored in CKD constantly, but epoetin does not affect it.
C — Creatinine: measures filtration; epoetin does nothing for kidney function.
D — Phosphorus: that evaluates phosphate binders, not epoetin.

2. A client has an indwelling urinary catheter. Which action is most important to prevent infection?
  1. Empty the drainage bag once each shift.
  2. Keep the drainage bag below the level of the bladder.
  3. Irrigate the catheter daily with sterile saline.
  4. Disconnect the tubing to obtain urine specimens.
Show the answer

B — Keep the bag below the bladder. Gravity keeps urine flowing away from the patient; if the bag rises above the bladder, contaminated urine refluxes back up the tubing into a sterile space. Also keep it off the floor and the tubing free of dependent loops.

A: too infrequent (empty at 1/2–2/3 full), and emptying frequency is not the main defense.
C: routine irrigation is never done — it breaks the closed system.
D: specimens come from the sampling port using aseptic technique; disconnecting tubing is a direct route for bacteria.

3. A nurse is teaching a client with AKI about fluid restriction. Which statement indicates understanding?
  1. "I should drink extra water if my urine is dark."
  2. "I will include ice chips as part of my daily fluid amount."
  3. "I can have unlimited soup because it is not a drink."
  4. "I should avoid weighing myself while on fluid restriction."
Show the answer

B — Ice chips count. Anything liquid at room temperature counts — ice, gelatin, popsicles, ice cream, soup, and the water taken with medications. Roughly 1 cup of ice equals 1/2 cup of water.

A: drinking extra defeats the restriction; dark urine in AKI reflects the kidney's failure, not thirst.
C: soup is mostly fluid and high in sodium — doubly wrong.
D: the opposite — daily weight is the best measure of whether the restriction is working.

4. A client is receiving peritoneal dialysis. Which finding is most concerning for peritonitis?

  3. Cloudy dialysate with abdominal pain
  4. Increased blood glucose after exchange

A: clear pale-yellow outflow is the normal expected finding.
B: mild fullness during dwell is expected — there are 2 liters of fluid in the abdomen.
D: expected — the dialysate contains dextrose, which is absorbed. Monitor glucose, especially in diabetics.

5. A nurse is teaching UTI prevention. Which statement indicates a need for further teaching?

7. Teaching a client with nephrotic syndrome. Which statement indicates a need for further teaching?

8. A client has a new AV graft in the forearm. Which technique should the nurse use to assess patency?

  3. Flush the graft with sterile saline.

A — Palpate for a thrill (and auscultate for a bruit). Both are noninvasive and directly confirm turbulent arterial flow through the access. Check every shift and teach the patient to check it daily.

B: never take a BP on the access arm — the cuff can clot it.
C: only dialysis staff access the graft; flushing risks infection and embolization.
D: no IVs, no sticks in that arm — period.

9. A client is 2 weeks post kidney transplant. Which finding suggests organ rejection?
  1. Increased urine output
  2. Decreased serum creatinine
  3. Fever and decreased urine output
  4. Mild incisional discomfort
Show the answer

C — Fever and decreased urine output. Acute rejection is an inflammatory attack on the graft: fever, falling urine output, rising creatinine, tenderness over the graft site, weight gain, hypertension and malaise. Report immediately — caught early, it is often reversible with high-dose steroids.

A — Increased urine output and B — Decreased creatinine are signs the graft is working.

10. A client reports severe flank pain, nausea, vomiting, and pink-tinged urine. Which action is the priority?
  1. Strain all urine.
  2. Administer prescribed analgesic.
  3. Encourage ambulation.
  4. Teach dietary changes to prevent recurrence.
Show the answer

B — Administer the prescribed analgesic. Renal colic is among the most severe pain there is, and the pain itself drives the tachycardia, hypertension and vomiting. The patient cannot drink, walk, or cooperate with anything until it is controlled.

A — Strain all urine: essential, and it happens with every void — but it does not relieve suffering.

11. Reviewing labs after hemodialysis. Which value should the nurse expect to decrease?
  1. BUN
  2. Hemoglobin
  3. Calcium
  4. Albumin
Show the answer

A — BUN. Urea is a small solute that diffuses readily across the dialyzer membrane — removing it is exactly what dialysis is for. Creatinine, potassium, phosphorus, weight and BP also fall.

B — Hemoglobin: unchanged or slightly lower — dialysis does not treat anemia (no erythropoietin).
C — Calcium: often stays the same or rises slightly; the dialysate contains calcium.
D — Albumin: too large a molecule to cross a hemodialysis membrane. (Protein is lost in peritoneal dialysis — a good contrast to know.)

12. Select all that apply. Which findings should the nurse expect in a client with end-stage renal disease?

  3. Fluid volume overload

  5. Metabolic acidosis
  6. Hypophosphatemia
Show the answer

A, B, C, E. Every one traces to a lost kidney function: potassium is not excreted (A), erythropoietin is not made (B), water is not excreted (C), and H⁺ is not excreted while bicarbonate is not regenerated (E).

14. A client has incomplete bladder emptying from urinary obstruction. Which complication is the nurse most concerned about?

  2. Postrenal acute kidney injury
  3. Nephrotic syndrome
  4. Respiratory alkalosis

Sudden severe LEFT flank pain radiating to the groin · restless, cannot get comfortable · nausea and vomiting · microscopic hematuria · temp 98.4°F

⭐ Must-Not-Forget Strip — the eight things

The repetition is deliberate. If you read nothing else the morning of the exam, read this.

  1. Potassium kills first. In every renal question, scan for K⁺ >5.0 and for peaked T waves. Sequence: calcium gluconate (protect) → insulin+D50/albuterol (shift) → dialysis or binder (remove).
  2. Crackles beat everything except a lethal rhythm. Fluid in the alveoli is a breathing problem. Sit them up, oxygen, call — then diurese or dialyze.
  3. Daily weight is the best fluid measure. 1 kg = 1 L. Same scale, same time, after voiding, same clothing.
  4. Know the AKI flip. Oliguric phase: overload, HIGH potassium, RESTRICT fluids. Diuretic phase: deficit, LOW potassium, REPLACE fluids. Same patient, opposite orders.
  5. Blood leaking vs. protein leaking. Cola urine + HTN + oliguria = glomerulonephritis. Foamy urine + massive edema + albumin <3 = nephrotic syndrome.
  6. Protect the fistula. Feel the thrill, hear the bruit, every shift. No BP, no IVs, no blood draws, nothing tight on that arm. No thrill = call now.
  7. Cloudy dialysate = peritonitis. It appears before the fever. Culture the effluent and notify immediately — this becomes sepsis.
  8. Relieve obstruction first, and cover the classic clues. Distended bladder + BPH + rising creatinine → scan and catheterize. Painless hematuria → bladder cancer, get the cystoscopy. Colicky pain + restless → stone: analgesia, strain all urine. New confusion in an elder → UTI. PKD → blood pressure is the whole game.

And the one question behind all of it

What is the kidney failing to do — filter, balance fluid, balance electrolytes, balance acid, control blood pressure, or make red cells? Name the broken job, and the assessment findings, the priority, and the complication you are preventing all fall out of it.
